# Kingdom of Nobodies — v0.5.3: The Hidden Hand

A mobile-first, low-poly 3D settlement that lives for five hundred years. Five strangers light a hearth.
Their descendants lay out streets, borrow from banks, join guilds, keep their faiths, trade with
neighbouring kingdoms — and, century by century, rebuild their timber village into brick, concrete,
steel and glass, while the old town they grew up in is kept. You are the council: you can nudge the
society, but you can't control anyone. And from Year 200 you are something else too: an unseen
presence who may leave a single carved stone, and let them make of it what they will.

**Play:** https://outerheavenx.github.io/kingdom-of-nobodies/ · version 0.5.3 · add it to your iPhone's
Home Screen to play it as an app.

## The Hidden Hand (v0.5.3)

You were always outside the kingdom. Now you can reach into it, a little. Nobody knows you are
there: you can't speak, appear, give orders or prove anything. What the villagers make of what
happens is up to them.

The rule from every earlier milestone still holds. The chronicle only reports what the simulation
did, and every coin moves through the ledger. Faith, doubt, missions, shrines and societies all come
from people's own reasoning, relationships and money. You never need the Hidden Hand to keep the
kingdom alive, and **doing nothing is never punished.**

### The Unseen Influence

The floating **eye** (bottom right) opens a dark emerald and gold panel. It is also in **Council →
The Unseen Influence**, where you can move the eye to the left or hide it. The panel has six sections:

- **Blessings**, **Signs & Omens**, **Prayers**, **Mysteries**, **Sacred Places** and the
  **Hidden Chronicle**. The Hidden Chronicle is your private record of what you actually did,
  next to what people saw and what they made of it.
- **Whispers** are what you spend. You start with 4 and can hold at most 12. They come back only
  with time (one every four days). Worship, prayer and temples never give you more.

### The Mysterious Stone (from Year 200)

At the start of Year 200 a private notice appears: *A Whisper Across the Ages*. You can choose
**Place the Mysterious Stone** or **Not Yet**. The offer never expires, and a save from after Year
200 offers it as soon as it loads.

- **Choose an inscription.** There are four in English (*Someone has always been watching.*, a
  question, light, silence) and one in glyphs only, written in an original invented script that
  nobody in the world can read.
- **Choose a place.** Pick one of six suggested sites (hilltop, forest clearing, meadow, market
  square, valley, beside history) or tap open land. A live preview shows where it will stand. It
  can't go on a road, a plot, water or the edge of the map. There is only ever one stone. Roads,
  plots and decorations keep clear of it afterwards, and it is saved with the kingdom.
- **No one is told.** Someone has to come across it: a forager, a traveller, someone who was told,
  or someone who read about it. After that, word spreads in conversation, in families, in
  newspapers (once communications technology exists) and through missions abroad.
- **Interpretations come from people.** Each person weighs what they saw, who told them, how
  sceptical, open and educated they are, and what their friends think. The views are: the work of
  an unseen god, a sign from the cosmos, the work of an ancient people, a prank, a natural or
  undecided puzzle, or not worth caring about. **Belief is never tied to intelligence or morality.**
  Education only makes indifference less likely, and honesty and generosity play no part. People
  change their minds slowly, and their interest fades if nothing happens.
- **What follows is earned.** Visits and gatherings turn into pilgrimages. Scholars run studies
  that move through eight findings. There are anniversaries every 25 years. The stone can become a
  landmark, and the council can pass a protection law. Works at the site cost real coin from a
  community, a society or the treasury, and wait for the right age: offerings, a fence, lanterns, a
  plinth, a pavilion, an observatory, a museum and a garden.

### Worldviews and faiths

Every person has a worldview: one of the existing traditions, a faith of the stone, the Path of
Reason, a cosmic view, agnostic, atheist or personal. The religion system from Milestone 5 was
extended, not duplicated:

- **The Way of the Hidden Hand** can form when convinced, interested people come together. A
  devout follower of an old tradition can keep it and honour the stone as well. Once it is large, it
  can split into the **Keepers of the Silence** and the **Children of the Light**.
- **Societies** form when enough people share an outlook and one of them organises: the **Rational
  Inquiry Society**, the **Seekers Beyond** and the **Ancestral Circle**. They collect dues, debate,
  watch the sky, keep rites, fund a scholar and run winter charity. Charity is never only
  religious.
- **Missionaries** travel only on real, open routes, never while there is a war, and need their
  provisions paid for. Named foreigners decide for themselves, and circles of believers can grow
  abroad. Journeys can go wrong. **No one is ever converted by force.**
- **Shrines, chapels and temples** are still built through the ordinary construction system, with
  real money and builders. In later ages they take later architectural forms, and a faith of the
  stone carries its emblem.
- **Politics:** a **Freedom of Belief Act** and a **Protection of the Carved Stone Act** can be
  proposed, debated and voted on like any other law. Societies can endorse candidates. This is
  advisory only: it doesn't directly change any votes.

### Prayers, blessings and signs

People pray about real circumstances: illness, a journey, a harvest, work, a business, grief, war,
needing courage, a companion, learning, gratitude and protection. You can answer with a blessing,
send a sign, or do nothing.

| Blessing | Whispers | Lasts | Effect (bounded) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 🍀 Gentle Fortune | 3 | 6 days | Slightly better odds of being hired, of a better yield from their own fields, or of trade at a struggling business |
| 🌿 The Healing Whisper | 4 | 5 days | A sick person is more likely to recover, and the illness is less dangerous. It can't cure everything |
| 🌾 Favorable Harvest | 5 | 6 days | Farms in a small area yield about a quarter more grain, and every extra sheaf is recorded |
| 🔥 A Moment of Courage | 2 | 3 days | Someone facing a hard choice feels bolder. What they decide is still theirs |
| 🧭 Safe Passage | 3 | 8 days | Slightly lower danger on a militia march or a missionary's road |
| 💡 Inspiration | 3 | 6 days | An apprentice, teacher, pupil or scholar learns faster |
| 🕊 Comfort | 2 | 4 days | Grief or low spirits weigh slightly less |
| ✨ A Chance Encounter | 3 | 2 days | Two strangers cross paths. What comes of it is up to them |
| 🌬 Peaceful Winds | 6 | 8 days | Tempers cool with a quarrelling kingdom. No ruler is made to sign anything |
| 🛡 Unseen Protection | 4 | 8 days | Slightly better odds against one present danger: illness, battle, hunger, fire or a bitter winter |

Each blessing also has a cooldown and its own rules about who can receive it. **People never learn
that a blessing happened.** They only notice what changed, and they explain it in their own ways:
the doctor, luck, prayer, or nothing at all.

**Signs** are visible in the 3D world: lights in the night sky, a glow around the stone, a double
rainbow (only after rain), a sudden field of flowers (not in winter), a musical tone, falling stars,
a strange cloud, or calm in a storm. Only the people who are nearby see a sign, and each of them
interprets it differently.

### Sights and sound

- **The stone** is procedurally carved. Its glyphs glow faintly at night, and it has cracks, moss,
  drifting motes and the site works around it. A placement preview shows before you confirm.
- **Signs** have their own effects, and blessed people show a faint shimmer that only you can see.
  Villagers pray, keep vigils, preach, debate and visit the stone.
- **Sound:** a short motif changes with the age, with a whisper, a chime, a temple bell and cues for
  omens, discovery and blessings. They play on the music and effects buses and follow the mixer, the
  reduced audio setting and the iPhone tap-to-enable rule. **Reduced motion** turns off the eye's
  motes and the notice animation.

### Saves (format 9)

A version 8 kingdom (v0.5.2) upgrades on load. It gains an empty divine layer, and every person is
given a worldview based on the faith they already hold. Nothing else changes. A save from after Year
200 offers the stone straight away. The cloud service accepts format 9, and it still refuses to let
an older game version overwrite a newer save.

### Known limitations (v0.5.3)

- The production build and the mobile end-to-end test run only in CI. This environment can't
  install Vite. The local browser check used a small development server and Playwright on an
  emulated iPhone 13, not a physical iPhone.
- A society's endorsement of a candidate is recorded and reported, but it doesn't change how anyone
  votes.
- The scripted player in the long-run test only places the stone, answers some prayers and sends
  the occasional sign. It doesn't use every blessing in every situation.
- The other kingdoms hold missions, named believers and circles, but their faiths aren't simulated
  in the same detail as the home kingdom's.

## The Evolution of Civilization (v0.5.2)

The rule from every earlier milestone holds: the chronicle only reports what the simulation did, and
every coin moves through the ledger. Nothing in this release is a cut-scene; a skyline appears only
when people decide to build it and can pay for it.

### Five ages

| Age | Opens | What becomes possible |
|---|---|---|
| 🏡 Age of Foundations | Year 1 | Timber, thatch and stone: cottages, farmsteads, workshops, taverns, a market |
| 🏭 Industrial Awakening | Year 101 | Brick townhouses, factories, warehouses, gaslight, piped water, railways |
| 🚗 Modern Expansion | Year 201 | Concrete, electricity, suburbs, apartments, motor cars, buses, a hospital, early high-rises |
| 🏙️ Metropolitan Age | Year 301 | Steel and glass: skyscrapers, condominiums, rapid transit, digital networks, renewables |
| 🌿 Age of Tomorrow | Year 401 | Solar glass and living walls, autonomous transit, robotics, vertical gardens |

The ages follow the existing calendar (a year is still twelve days), so ageing, inheritance,
elections, production and the holidays are unchanged. **An age only makes things possible.** On the
morning of Year 101 every building is exactly as it was the night before.

### Technology

A data-driven registry of 25 technologies (`src/civilization/Technology.js`). Each has an age, the
technologies it needs, and local needs read from the simulation — a working smithy for steam boilers,
sixty townspeople and brick buildings already standing for a railway, four modern buildings before
steel-and-glass. The council adopts one at a time, at most once a season and every few years, and pays
real coins to the wider world for it. If the treasury can't pay (with a reserve), the technology waits.
New architecture is buildable only once its technology is adopted.

### Utilities and infrastructure

Power, water, communications and transit are simplified networks with a level the town's technology
allows and a level actually laid in each district. The council lays them district by district as
public works, paid day by day to local crews (or outside contractors), with yearly upkeep; when upkeep
can't be paid, a district's service falls back. Buildings that need more than their district provides
(towers need mains water and bus routes, for example) work at reduced capacity — fewer beds, less
output — until the network catches up.

### Architecture

Every building keeps its function (`cottage`, `bakery`, `lumberyard`…) and records the era it was
built or last rebuilt in, a deterministic seed and its storeys. The era decides its look, value, beds
and staff. `src/civilization/ArchitectureData.js` defines the forms for every type in every era, e.g.

- **Homes:** timber-frame / stone / thatched cottage → brick townhouse / Victorian house → suburban
  house → contemporary family home → solar villa.
- **Lumberyard:** lumber workshop → brick sawmill → industrial warehouse → corporate office tower →
  automated construction campus. (Smithy → ironworks → machine works → manufacturing plant →
  automated production facility; merchant shop → department store → shopping centre → shopping
  complex → smart market hall; tavern → coaching inn → hotel → hotel tower → sky hotel; and civic
  buildings, banks, guild halls, schools, granaries, wells, chapels and farms likewise.)
- **New types:** apartment blocks (brick tenement → concrete flats → condominiums → garden apartments),
  residential towers (early high-rise → glass skyscraper → vertical garden tower / solar spire),
  mansions (manor house → Victorian mansion → luxury estate → modern mansion → futuristic villa) with
  their grounds, railway stations and hospitals.

Footprints are era-aware and shared by navigation, decorations and the renderer, so what blocks a
villager is what is drawn. Zoning follows distance from the crossroads: only the centre may build tall,
the suburbs stay low and farmland never sprouts towers. Land in the centre gains value as the town
becomes a city. Buildings wear slowly with age (newer materials more slowly) and are worth less in
poor repair.

### Modernization and redevelopment

- **Owners decide.** Every few days a handful of owners review their buildings. A merchant whose timber
  workshop is decades old and whose trade is good may pull it down for a brick factory; a family that
  has held a house for generations is slow to; a poor household keeps the cottage it has. Wealth, bank
  loans, condition, age, profitability, crowding, personality and the district's utilities all count.
- **A rebuild is a real project:** demolition first (salvage goes to the owner, the land is cleared and
  the history records it), then foundations, structure, roof and facade, and fittings. Builders are
  paid by the owner, timber bought locally, and modern materials (brick, concrete, steel, glass)
  bought from the town's builders' merchant — a trading lumberyard or smithy, so Bram's lumberyard can
  grow rich supplying the city. From the Modern age a contracted crew keeps big sites moving when no
  local builder comes. A business keeps trading through its rebuild and gains staff in modern premises.
- **Developers** build apartments and towers to rent when families need homes, and wealthy families
  commission mansions. Every site is acquired legally: a plot on the market, or an owner who neither
  lives nor trades there and accepts a generous offer. Towers and mansions need a second, neighbouring
  plot (a plaza or estate grounds) — land consolidation. Rent is a coin a day per household, paid to the
  landlord (to the treasury for council flats, which charge only working households and never evict).

### Heritage, preservation and Then & Now

- Buildings gather **historic importance** from age, the founders' time, long-lived businesses, being a
  public landmark or the first building of an era. The Founders' Memorial is always protected.
- A scheme that would pull down an important building (or raise a tower beside a listed one) goes to
  **planning**. If enough villagers care — people who are loyal, old, live nearby, descend from its
  owners or keep the Hearthkin faith — they campaign, and the council votes, weighing heritage against
  growth and housing need.
- Once enough has been lost or fought over, the government may pass the **Heritage Preservation Act**.
  Under it the council lists the most important buildings each year and declares **conservation
  areas** (the old town first, later the oldest surviving quarter) where old buildings may be repaired
  but not pulled down and no towers may rise; campaigners can take an approved scheme to **court**,
  where the judge weighs the building's documented importance and may grant an injunction.
- **Then & Now.** Every property keeps a compact, permanent record (type, era, seed, storeys, owner,
  business) of every change, taken within the hour it happens. Open a property → *Then & Now* to see its
  timeline, choose any year, and see that year's building reconstructed in 3D beside today's with a
  before/after divider you slide across the plot. Owners are listed with their family links ("child of
  the previous owner"). It is visual only: time, ownership and buildings are untouched (tested byte for
  byte).

### Transport

Long trips across town are made by car (for households with savings), bus or rapid transit (where the
district has the service) and later autonomous pods: the villager follows the same pathfinder route, only
faster, and is drawn in that vehicle. Caravans travel by freight rail (once a station is built), lorry or
automated cargo, faster on the regional roads, and are drawn as what they travel in. With motor cars the
council resurfaces the busiest roads to asphalt (and later smart roads), which is also cheaper to walk.

### Graphics

- Procedural architecture for every later era, assembled from reusable parts (walls, gable, hip,
  mansard and sawtooth roofs, chimneys and smokestacks, balconies, shopfronts, columns, porches,
  gardens, pools, gates, solar panels, green roofs, rooftop plant, cranes) with several silhouettes for
  skyscrapers (setbacks, slabs, twisting, tapering, round and garden towers) and spires, helipads and
  aviation lights.
- Facades are procedural textures (brick, factory glazing, concrete, Art Deco, glass curtain wall,
  eco panels, solar glass) that repeat by storey and bay, with windows that light up at night. Glass
  reflects a small pre-filtered sky (off in battery-saver mode).
- Street lighting follows the power network: torches, gas lamps, electric, LED, then light rings, lining
  every street in later ages. Asphalt roads have kerbs and centre lines; smart roads glow.
- Construction sites in later ages raise a concrete or steel frame storey by storey, with a slewing
  tower crane for tall work.
- Seasons and holidays keep working with every era: snow settles on factory and tower roofs, and
  decorations are placed around each building's real footprint.

### Sound and music

A Web Audio engine in `src/audio/`, with everything synthesised live in the browser — no recordings and
no borrowed music.

- **An original soundtrack** composed from descriptions in `MusicLibrary.js`: at least two pieces per
  age (day and night), e.g. *Hearthsong* and *Moonlit Commons* (lute, harp, flute, frame drum), *Brass
  and Steam* and *Gaslight Waltz*, *Main Street Swing* (swing, walking bass, brushes) and *Evening
  Radio*, *Glass Horizon* and *Night Skyline*, *Solar Gardens* and *Starlit Transit*, plus themes for
  the title screen, festivals, winter, New Year, war, disasters and a fanfare for great moments.
- **The music director** chooses from the age, the hour, war, festivals, New Year, fires and the
  kingdom on screen (each kingdom hears it in its own key), crossfades over a few seconds, and never
  flaps between calm cues.
- **Ambience** is mixed from what is around the point you look at: wind, birds, crowds, market
  chatter, horses, carts, hammering at real building sites, anvils, steam, machinery, traffic, trains,
  trams, quiet electric pods, rain and snowy wind. The surroundings' own age is heard: the preserved old
  town still sounds medieval in the Age of Tomorrow.
- **Effects** for taps, panels, selection, construction, demolition, coins, notifications, fireworks
  and celebrations, placed by distance and direction from the view, with cooldowns and a voice limit so
  a crowd of builders is a rhythm, not a din. Zoom in on a smithy and you hear the hammer; zoom out and
  it fades into the town.
- **Mixer:** master, music, ambience, effects and interface sliders with mutes, shuffle and a reduced
  audio mode, kept on this device (not in the save).
- **iPhone-safe:** nothing is created or played until you tap *Enable sound* (on the title screen, the
  🔇 button or Council → Sound & music). Sound suspends when the game is hidden and resumes only on your
  next tap — it never starts playing by itself. Add `?dev` to the address for an audio test panel.

### iPhone app

- An original app icon — a gold crown over a skyline that runs from a medieval keep to glass towers and
  a garden spire, on forest green — as an SVG source (`public/icons/icon.svg`) and PNGs rasterised with
  `node scripts/build-icons.mjs`: 1024, 512 and 192 (any), 512 and 192 (maskable, in the safe zone),
  180 (Apple touch icon) and a 32 px favicon. All opaque RGB, as iOS expects.
- A web app manifest (standalone, theme colours, icons) with paths relative to the Pages base.
- A title screen with the icon, a medieval-to-future skyline, real loading progress, the build version,
  *Start* / *Continue · Year N* and an *Enable sound* switch; load failures show a reload button.
- A service worker generated with each build: a cache named after the build, old caches deleted on
  activation, pages fetched network-first (so a new deployment is never hidden behind a stale page),
  hashed assets cache-first. It never touches the saves. The game starts offline once installed.
- **To install:** open the game in Safari → Share → *Add to Home Screen*. It launches full screen, with
  the safe areas and Dynamic Island respected, in portrait or landscape.

### Settings and interface

- The **era chip** under the date opens *World → Era & tech*: the age and the years to the next, every
  technology with what it needs and costs, utilities district by district, public works and transport.
- *World → Evolution*: buildings by era, modernization, landmarks and conservation areas, density,
  housing and rented flats, mansions, the tallest and most valuable buildings, redevelopment under way,
  planning applications and disputes.
- Each property shows its architecture, age, condition, value, utilities and heritage, and *Then & Now*.
- *Council → Graphics*: Low / Medium / High, see-through towers, adaptive resolution.

### Performance

Static parts of each building are merged into one mesh per material (geometry batching), tall
buildings get a distant stand-in (level of detail), street lights are batched, tall buildings fade to
glass when they hide the villager you follow, rebuilds after a redevelopment wave are spread over
several frames, adaptive resolution steps down when frames are slow, and battery-saver mode drops
shadows, reflections and distant villagers.

### Saves

Save format 8. A version 7 (Milestone 5.1) or older kingdom upgrades on load: every standing building
becomes Age of Foundations architecture with a deterministic seed, the world joins the age its calendar
has reached with no technology adopted beyond timber framing, utilities start unbuilt and each
property's history begins with what stands on it. No coins, goods, people or dates change. **The
original save is kept on the device before it is upgraded** (Council → *Pre-upgrade copy* downloads it).
The cloud service refuses to let an older game version overwrite a kingdom saved in the newer format.

### Known limitations (v0.5.2)

- Physical iPhone installation was **not** tested on a device: the PWA, audio unlock, orientation,
  offline start and touch flows were verified in Chromium emulating an iPhone 13 (WebKit's own audio
  and Home Screen behaviour may differ in details).
- Frame rates were measured only under software rendering in CI-like containers, which says little
  about an iPhone 15 Pro Max; the adaptive resolution and Low preset exist for slower devices.
- The population stays at village-to-town scale (about 80–160 simulated people) because every person is
  simulated individually; towers house these people, there is no abstract city population.
- Heritage disputes go to the council and the courts, but there is no player action to list a building
  directly.
- The optional cloud backup is still implemented but not deployed (see below).


## Seasons & Celebrations (Milestone 5.1)

The valley now changes with the year, and you can tell the season or holiday just by looking at it.

- **Spring:** vivid green grass, blossom on the trees, flower beds by the doors, butterflies, young crops,
  warm light and spring showers.
- **Summer:** deep greens and bright sun, flowers and pennants, full-grown crops, drifting clouds, and
  fireflies in the evening.
- **Autumn:** orange, gold and red trees (each tree its own shade), drifting leaves, leaves on the road
  verges, golden fields, hay bales and pumpkin patches on the farms, and golden light.
- **Winter:** snow falls and settles on upward-facing surfaces: ground, roofs, fences, trees, wall tops and
  stalls. It lies in patches rather than one white sheet, and roads take much less so they stay readable.
  How much lies, and how fast it melts, comes from the simulation's own weather of the last twelve days;
  there is no second climate model. Also frost, cold morning mist and lit windows on long evenings.

Colours ease over the last fifth of each season and over a few seconds when something changes, so
there are no hard cuts.

### The holidays

The kingdom's year starts with spring, so New Year's midnight is the real moment its year number
advances. Holidays are placed by their part of the season rather than by month, so a calendar with
shorter or longer seasons keeps them all in order.

| Holiday | When (kingdom calendar) | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| 🎆 New Year's Eve / Day | Winter 3, 14:00 → Spring 1, 10:00 | Lanterns, banners and lights; a countdown in the top bar; fireworks at midnight, then confetti |
| 💗 Valentine's Day | Spring 1 | Pink blossom on every tree, heart garlands, heart posts, romantic benches, flowers, floating hearts |
| ☘️ St. Patrick's Day | Spring 2, morning | Green bunting, shamrocks in the grass, a rainbow with a pot of gold, green lanterns |
| 🥚 Spring Festival | Spring 2–3 | Painted eggs, egg baskets, flower arches, flower beds, pastel bunting, blossom |
| 🔥 Summer Solstice | Summer 1–2 | Bonfires with rising sparks, flower wreaths on doors, colourful bunting, lanterns, fireflies |
| 🎏 Founders' Day | Summer 3 | Kingdom flags along the roads, a parade float, fireworks at 21:00 |
| 🌽 Harvest Festival | Autumn 1–2 | Hay bales, corn stalks, pumpkins, scarecrows, harvest carts, harvest displays |
| 🎃 Halloween | Autumn 2 night → Autumn 3 | Pumpkins and glowing jack-o'-lanterns by homes, roads, the square and farms; waving skeletons; friendly ghosts; bats; scarecrows; cobwebs; spooky trees; orange and purple lanterns; haunted stalls; purple ground fog |
| 🦃 Gratitude Feast | Autumn 3 night → Winter 1 | Long food tables, autumn wreaths, harvest displays |
| 🎄 Winter Festival | Winter 1–3 | A great decorated tree with gifts, smaller trees, wreaths on doors, ribbon posts, coloured lights, warm lanterns, holiday stalls, falling snow |

**Every kingdom celebrates in its own way.**
- Greenvale ties ribbons to its windmills and fills its fields.
- Ironhold's walled mountain town keeps a winter blanket of snow, and lines its walls with lanterns.
- Saltmere hangs lanterns along its docks, dresses its ships, and sets off fireworks over the water. Its
  mild coast sees sleet where First Hearth gets snow.

Kingdoms you are not visiting keep no decoration state; it is all derived when you arrive.

**Placement.**
- Decorations are seeded by holiday and place, so the same pumpkins sit on the same steps after a reload.
- They stand beside doors (never in front of them), in yard corners, on road verges (never on the road),
  around the square and in the fields. Wreaths hang on doors and cobwebs on eaves.
- A test checks every ground decoration of every set against roads and buildings. New districts are
  decorated as they are built.
- All of it is built from shared low-poly parts, drawn as one instanced mesh per part type. The
  decorations are faded out and their GPU resources released when a holiday ends.

**Citizens take part by choice.** Celebrating is one more option weighed against work, food and sleep.
- The hungry eat first, and workers finish their shifts.
- Couples meet at the sweethearts' benches on Valentine's Day.
- Friends exchange handmade gifts at the Winter Festival.
- Some stay up for the New Year's countdown in the square.
- Children go lantern-visiting at Halloween.
- On market days, people with coins to spare buy a treat at an open stall: an ordinary sale.

The Spring and Winter Festivals are feasts of particular faith traditions (Dawn and Furrow, Hearthkin and
Lantern). Their followers lean in, devout followers of other traditions may keep their own observances,
and sceptics come for the lights. Celebrations create no coins or goods. The chronicle records each New
Year, and a short log of each celebration is kept: how many came, couples and gifts.

### Settings, preview and accessibility

All of these are in **Council → Seasons & celebrations** (also reachable from the World tab).

- **Holiday Preview:** see any season or any holiday right now, by day or by night, with fireworks again
  on demand. It changes only the picture: simulation time, ages, money, births and deaths, festivals, the
  chronicle and the save are untouched (tested byte for byte). *Exit preview* returns immediately.
- **Holidays the kingdom keeps:** a switch for each one. This is saved with the kingdom; a holiday that is
  off is neither decorated nor celebrated.
- **Graphics:** Low / Medium / High quality. On a phone:
  - Low means no shadows, at most about 260 particles, and fewer decorations.
  - Medium means lighter shadows.
  - High means full resolution and soft shadows.
- **Display toggles:** holiday decorations and effects, snow particles, fireworks, seasonal atmosphere,
  and decoration density.
- **Reduced motion:** Auto (follows the system setting), On or Off. On means fewer and slower particles,
  gentle fireworks without bursts or flashes, and calmer ghosts and bats.
- **Timing:**
  - Holiday length (short / normal / long).
  - A minimum time each holiday's look stays on screen, so a holiday doesn't flash past at 20× speed.
  - *Match my calendar* shows today's real season and holiday. This is visual only: the kingdom's own
    time is unchanged.
- **Bells at New Year:** synthesised, and off by default.
- **Popups** now stay about 8 seconds (holidays 10), show a draining timer bar, pause while you hold one,
  and close when tapped.
- **Palette:** the interface uses a brighter palette of deep royal indigo with sunflower gold, coral and
  teal accents.

**Performance.** Particles (snow, leaves, petals, confetti, hearts, sparks, butterflies, fireflies,
fireworks) are animated entirely on the GPU, in a box that follows the camera. The CPU only sets a clock
and a centre each frame. The theme is checked twice a second, never every frame.

## The Age of Empires & Prosperity (Milestone 5)

The rule from earlier milestones holds: the chronicle only reports what the simulation actually did, and
every coin moves through the ledger. Money is still conserved exactly. Banks, guilds and faith communities
have ledger accounts of their own, and the stress test checks the books.

- **The town plans itself.** The four inner districts keep their hand-drawn layout. After that, the town
  grows street by street.
  - The planners measure real demand:
    - families squeezed into the communal hall;
    - homelessness and growth;
    - people per shop;
    - empty land;
    - traffic.
  - They search for the best street that branches at right angles off an existing road. A street is
    rejected if it would cross the river, a building, a plot or another road, or leave the valley.
  - Each plan goes to the council with its costs (road, survey) and benefits (beds, shops, land value).
    The council approves it, cuts it down to fit the treasury, borrows the gap from a bank against the land
    it will open, or rejects it.
  - The works crew builds the street shift by shift. Its plots are surveyed only once it is finished, so
    every new home has a road to its door (tested with the pathfinder).
  - Districts record how their character changes, for example from a residential quarter to a market
    quarter. The council rezones idle land toward what is short.
  - Citizens with capital (or a loan) build cottages to sell, but only while families need homes and some
    of them could afford the price. Only two such cottages are under way at a time. Families can buy a
    finished cottage and move in.
- **Kingdoms you can visit.** Kingdoms → *Visit in 3D* takes the camera to Greenvale, Ironhold or Saltmere.
  Only one settlement is drawn at a time, and the simulation keeps running everywhere.
  - Each kingdom has its own scene:
    - **Greenvale**: rolling farmland with windmills.
    - **Ironhold**: a walled stone town with mines and a smelter under the mountains.
    - **Saltmere**: a harbour with docks, boats, warehouses and a lighthouse.
  - Each kingdom has about 25 persistent named people with stable ids: a ruler, councillors, merchants, a
    guild master, a faith keeper, craftspeople and residents. They age, marry, have children, change
    trades, rise and fall with their kingdom, migrate along the real roads (taking days, never
    teleporting) and die. Vacant roles are filled by promotion.
  - Rulers are named citizens. Their character (ambition, caution, warmth, pride) shapes their priorities,
    how they negotiate and whether they go to war. Their record lists the treaties and wars of their reign.
  - Foreign merchant houses send a named merchant with each caravan, and that merchant is away until the
    caravan comes home. Carts in a kingdom's market are the caravans actually stopped there. Tents outside
    its walls are an army actually camped there.
- **Banks, credit and loans.** All banks keep a real balance sheet: cash, loans, deposits and equity.
  - The banks are moneylenders, merchant banks, the council's savings house and guild credit funds.
  - A deposit moves coins into the bank and records what the bank owes. You can only withdraw what you
    deposited, and only what the bank actually holds in cash.
  - Credit is assessed from:
    - income and how long the borrower has worked;
    - business profit;
    - repayment history;
    - existing debts;
    - collateral;
    - reputation.

    A rich idler with past defaults is refused, while a steady smith is not.
  - Loans pay for premises, homes, business expansion and development. The council can also borrow, for
    streets and for grain in a famine.
  - Interest accrues once a season, in whole coins, and never twice across a save and reload. Deposit
    interest is paid out of what the bank's loans earn, so idle savings cannot bankrupt it.
  - Missed payments bring penalties, then one restructuring, then default. A default goes to court under
    the Debt Recovery Act, and the judgment is carried out once:
    - repayment from the borrower's means;
    - or a forced sale of the pledged land, which repays the lender first;
    - or bankruptcy, with the loss written off and the borrower's credit ruined for ten years.
  - A failing bank is wound up in an orderly way. A healthy bank takes over its accounts and honours
    deposits as far as the failed bank's assets cover them. Without one, depositors share its cash pro
    rata.
  - Deaths and emigration settle debts and savings.
- **Guilds.** Merchants', Builders', Blacksmiths' and Carpenters' guilds and the Farmers' Association form
  once a trade has three practitioners.
  - Members pay dues into a real fund and elect a master.
  - The guild trains members, raises the quality of their shops, helps members who are out of work, and
    builds a hall (which raises the price of land nearby). Large guilds open credit funds and sponsor
    public works.
  - At elections, guilds back the candidate whose platform suits their trade, and their members' votes
    follow. A guild whose candidate wins may be granted a privilege, such as a lower market levy. Rival
    guilds that back opposing candidates are recorded as a dispute.
- **Faith.** Five original traditions:
  - **The Old Furrow**: nature and harvest.
  - **The Hearthkin**: ancestors and family memory.
  - **The Dawnlight**: light and renewal.
  - **The Open Hand**: service and charity.
  - **The Quiet Lantern**: scholarship and contemplation.

  Each villager is devout, casually affiliated, sceptical or holds to none, and that can change over a
  lifetime. Belief never affects honesty, intelligence, temper or worth.

  Communities collect offerings and pay for upkeep and a keeper. They raise shrines, chapels and temples
  and keep their seasonal rites. They also:
  - buy food from the village's own shops for a winter charity table, and in any hunger crisis;
  - sit with the bereaved;
  - sponsor the school;
  - help fire victims rebuild;
  - keep a book of the village's memorable days.

  People remember who helped them.
- **Alliances and war.**
  - **Disputes.** Three contested lands lie along real roads. Tension over each rises and falls for recorded
    reasons: relations, toll quarrels, need, embargoes, a ruler's pride. A formal dispute is negotiated
    every season.
  - **Deciding on war.** War is considered only after talks have failed twice. The ruler must be willing,
    the treasury must allow it, and the aggressor must expect to win. Defensive alliances can deter a war
    (the Chronicle says when they do).
  - **Allies.** When war is declared, allies must join or break their treaty. First Hearth can be drawn in
    through an alliance; its militia is made of real villagers who leave their jobs.
  - **Armies.** Mobilisation takes workers out of a kingdom's fields and mines. Armies carry real food,
    are paid every day, and march by the hour along the roads. Hunger costs them morale and deserters.
  - **Battles** weigh numbers, training, equipment, morale, supply, the commander and the ground, with a
    ±15% roll for the fortunes of the day.
  - **Sieges** close a town's roads to trade. They end in surrender, withdrawal, a parley or an assault.
  - **Peace.** Exhaustion, defeats and a three-year limit push both sides toward peace. The treaty sets
    who holds the land, any reparations, a non-aggression pact or ceasefire, and a recognised border.
  - **At home.** War blocks trade between the belligerents, sends refugees to First Hearth, and moves
    transit tolls with the territory. Any battle can be replayed on stylised ground.
- **Weather.** Clear, rain, fog, wind and snow follow the seasons and tend to persist from day to day.
  - Rain waters the crops in the growing months, while snow and fog slow the roads.
  - Wind fans fires, and a clear day lifts people's spirits.
  - The renderer draws whatever the simulation decided.
- **Optional cloud backup.** A Cloudflare Worker with a D1 database, GitHub sign-in, revision history,
  conflict detection and server-side validation (see [cloud/README.md](cloud/README.md)). It is
  implemented and tested in-process, but **not deployed**: it needs secrets that only the owner can
  create. Until then the Cloud card says it is not set up, and local saving is unchanged.
- **Look and feel.** New buildings: a counting house, guild halls, shrines, chapels, temples and barracks.
  - Houses have ridge beams, door frames, window sills and shutters.
  - There are rain and snow particles, overcast light and fog.
  - Militia muster at the north gate.
  - The camera has an overview and smooth zoom.
  - The navigation was rebuilt (see below).
  - Three.js is a separately cached chunk, so a game update does not re-download the engine.

## Beyond the Hearth (Milestone 4)

As before, the chronicle only reports what the simulation actually did: if it says a caravan delivered
grain, the grain moved, and so did the coins.

- **Roads and pathfinding.** Villagers walk on a 2-unit navigation grid built from the real building
  footprints, the river and the roads.
  - Routes come from A\* (8 neighbours, a binary heap), smoothed by cost-aware string-pulling and cached per
    layout. When a building goes up or burns down, routes replan.
  - An unreachable destination fails safely: the villager remembers it and picks something else to do.
  - Road surfaces set the walking cost: footpath, dirt, stone, a bridge, or wading the ford.
  - Roads are proposed for real reasons: owners petitioning for access, desire paths worn by heavy foot
    traffic, paving the busiest dirt road, or a bridge once the ford is crowded. The council approves a
    proposal and pays for it, and the works crew builds it over several shifts using timber and stone.
- **The region.** Three kingdoms surround First Hearth: **Greenvale** (farms), **Ironhold** (mines and forges)
  and **Saltmere** (a fishing port).
  - Each is simulated in aggregate:
    - population, prosperity and food cover;
    - production with input chains (ore → iron → tools) that follows the seasons;
    - spoilage;
    - a leader whose stance shifts with circumstances;
    - prices bounded between 0.45× and 3× of base.
  - They are linked by six roads that differ in terrain and quality. One of them is hidden until it is
    scouted.
  - The Region tab has an SVG map with fog of war. Expeditions led by a real villager, who is away while
    travelling, reveal corridors and landmarks.
- **Trade.** Merchants look for opportunities and explain them, for example: *"Ironhold is short of food;
  Greenvale sells at 1.51 a unit, Ironhold pays 3.45; 39 hours on the road; expected profit 79 coins"*.
  These merchants are First Hearth villagers who buy carts at the lumberyard, plus houses from each kingdom.
  - A contract holds the buyer's payment in escrow and reserves the seller's stock (a local seller's goods
    are physically set aside).
  - A caravan with a capacity loads the goods, pays drovers and tolls, and travels the regional roads in
    simulated time. It is slowed by winter, storms and heavy loads. It waits out floods and landslides,
    re-routes, or turns back.
  - Perishable cargo spoils. On delivery, escrow pays the merchant less the tariff, and the buyer's stores
    grow.
  - Every unit loaded ends up delivered, sold, spoiled or still aboard (this is checked).
  - Carts are drawn on the village road between the gate and the market. Tap one to follow it.
- **Diplomacy.** Every change in a relationship has a recorded cause: profitable trade, high tariffs,
  quarrels over tolls, gifts, embargoes, or old grievances fading with time.
  - Treaties change the actual rules:
    - trade treaties lower tariffs;
    - transit treaties waive tolls;
    - mutual assistance sends grain in a famine;
    - non-aggression pacts prevent embargoes.
  - Embargoes void contracts that haven't loaded yet and turn caravans back at the border. After one is
    lifted, neither side imposes another for three years. Feuds cost a kingdom's leader support.
  - People migrate between the settlements as identifiable individuals. An emigrant can come home with the
    same identity and family links.
- **Law and courts.** A legal code with enactment and effective dates. Founding laws cover property, wages,
  theft, fraud and tariffs; the government proposes and votes on new laws for contracts, licensing,
  building safety, permits and idle land.
  - Claims come from real events, with evidence captured at the time: missing wage payments with the
    employment record, a theft with its witnesses, a short delivery, a disputed estate, an embezzlement found
    in the ledger.
  - Cases are filed, scheduled and heard by an appointed judge. Courthouse capacity creates a backlog.
  - A judgment is executed exactly once through the ledger: restitution, a fine, an enforced contract, a
    property transfer or a licence suspension.
- **Seasons and culture.** Farms yield most in autumn and least in winter, and winter raises appetite.
  - Each season has a festival: the Festival of Renewal, the Midsummer Fair (which invites foreign
    merchants), the Harvest Festival and the Hearthlight Gathering. The council budgets real coins for each,
    a family may sponsor it, and food is bought from real sellers.
  - Villagers decide for themselves whether to attend, based on company, loneliness, work they still owe
    and distance. They eat from the feast and buy treats at the stalls.
  - Traditions grow out of history: a family that sponsors two harvests in a row starts a feast,
    Founders' Day begins once the last founder dies, there is a remembrance after a great fire, a
    merchants' market, and tales told by travellers. Customs from the kingdoms spread through contact.

## Earlier milestones: across the generations (Milestone 3)

Every story in the chronicle comes from the simulation itself. If the chronicle says something happened,
the state behind it changed.

- **Time and life.** A year has four seasons of three days (a year is about 7 minutes at 1×, or 22 seconds at
  20×). Every citizen has a birth day. Age, life stage (infant, child, teen, young adult, adult, elder) and
  birthdays all follow from it. Mortality rises with age (a Gompertz curve) and also with hunger, illness
  and accidents. Death is processed once: the job ends, the spouse is widowed, kin mourn, wards are
  re-homed, the estate is settled, and the person moves to the archive with their full biography.
- **Families.** Attraction, courtship, engagement, marriage at the Hearth Hall, divorce and widowhood.
  Couples decide whether they want children. Pregnancy lasts three seasons. Children inherit blended
  personality traits and appearance with some variation. Orphans become council wards and may be adopted.
  Family trees are acyclic by construction, and every house (family name) is ranked by its wealth, land,
  businesses, terms in office and achievements.
- **Education and careers.** Children take lessons at home, or at the school once one is built. Teens choose a
  trade and apprentice under a master, and mentors pass on skills. Skills grow only through practice: work
  shifts, lessons and apprenticeships. Elders retire and hand over their business.
- **Inheritance and succession.** Coins are split between the spouse and children. Land and buildings pass to the
  main heir, and so do unfinished construction projects. A business goes to the best-suited heir, is held in
  trust for a minor, or is sold. Nothing is duplicated: the money supply is conserved across every death
  (this is tested).
- **Government.** Once ten adults live in the settlement, it elects a Village Elder (later a Mayor, a Lord Mayor and finally a Lord Protector).
  The election runs like this:
  - Candidates campaign on platforms built from the issues they care about. Factions (the Merchant Guild, the
    Workers' Coalition, the Landowners' Association, the Traditionalists and the Reformers) endorse candidates.
  - Every adult casts a ballot at the hall during the campaign. The tally is deterministic, and a term lasts
    four years.
  - Ten policies change real variables: taxes and the tithe, food assistance, public housing, unemployment
    relief, the public works crew, market fees, construction grants, business licensing, public schooling
    and apprenticeship grants.
  - Approval responds to how people are living. Leaders can embezzle and be caught, protests can force a
    resignation, and a crisis makes the leader restore food assistance.
- **Population and the town.** Births, deaths, immigration (families of one to four people, drawn by jobs,
  housing and happiness) and emigration (after a long stretch of unhappiness).
  - Housing is a real constraint: families are rehoused, and public cottages are built.
  - When land runs short, the council pays road crews to open a new district, up to ten.
  - Buildings can be expanded (up to level 3), damaged, repaired, burned to ruins and rebuilt.
  - Civic infrastructure appears as the town grows: wells, a granary, a school and a Town Hall.
- **Events.** Fires that spread and are fought by neighbours (a well helps), crop failures, harsh winters, fever
  outbreaks and recessions. Six discoveries (the heavy plough, tempered tools, timber framing, sealed
  granaries, the watermill, clean wells and drains) change production and health permanently.
- **History.** Named eras (The Founding Years, The Age of Councils, The Garrow Dynasty, The Great Fire of
  Year 165…) are chosen from what each year actually brought. There is a searchable timeline by year, a
  biography for everyone who ever lived, and the **Founders' Memorial** in the square. Tap it to open the
  founders' stories. It gains a gold crown once all five founders have died.

## Architecture

The simulation is plain JavaScript over a single JSON state object with no DOM or WebGL dependencies, so it
runs identically in the browser and in Node tests. It advances in fixed 10-minute ticks, while rendering runs
at the display's frame rate. `index.html` contains no game logic.


Key design rules:

- **Systems change state, then record it.** Achievements, biographies and eras are derived from recorded
  facts.
- **Deterministic**: the RNG lives in the saved state. A reloaded world continues exactly, including mid-birth,
  mid-election and mid-fire (this is tested), and ¼×, 6× and 20× runs are identical to a 1× run. Between ticks, villagers
  and vehicles glide at a steady pace to their next position (`src/world/Glide.js`), so slow speeds don't stutter.
- **Bounded growth**: each year, compaction keeps each person's 24 most significant relationships, reduces
  closed businesses to compact records, and trims archive records to their identity, family, career highlights
  and achievements. The archive keeps every person who ever lived.
- **Money is a closed ledger.** The three kingdoms, escrow and the outside world are accounts too, so the
  total never changes: it is checked every simulated year.
- **Performance** for up to 250 citizens:
  - Lookups are cached by id. Expensive social summaries are computed once per day.
  - Villagers far from the view are hidden, and those at middle distance are moved but not animated.
  - Only the nearest ~14 unoverlapping name labels are drawn, plus the selected person's.
  - Buildings rebuild only when their state signature changes.
  - *Battery saver* lowers the pixel ratio, turns off shadows and tightens the level of detail.
  - The kingdoms are simulated once a day in aggregate rather than as people. The navigation grid is
    rebuilt only when the layout changes, and routes are cached per layout version.
  - **Web Worker: evaluated, not adopted.** A 200-year run takes about 1.0–1.2 ms per simulated tick at
    about 150 villagers in Node (see below). At 20× speed (80 ticks a second) that is about 10% of a second,
    well within budget even if a phone is several times slower. Moving the simulation into a worker would
    mean copying or proxying the whole state for every panel and pick, and would split saving and
    determinism across two threads. That cost is not worth paying at this scale.

### Saves

Saves are checksummed envelopes (format version 9) stored in IndexedDB:

- Every write keeps the previous save in the same transaction.
- Once per game year, a **recovery snapshot** is also kept, which you can restore from the Council tab.
- A copy goes to localStorage.
- Loading picks the newest valid copy.

Older saves migrate automatically.

- **A Milestone 5 kingdom (version 6)** gains the holiday calendar: every holiday kept, an empty
  celebration log and no fireworks recorded (so none are replayed). Nothing else changes.
  `tests/fixtures/m5-save.json` is a real save produced by the v0.5.0 code; a test migrates it and runs it
  on with money conserved. Decorations are never saved: they are derived from the calendar and seeds.
  Display settings (preview, density, reduced motion and so on) are kept on the device with the other
  graphics settings.

- **A Milestone 4 kingdom (version 5)** gains the city planner (its existing districts become named
  districts), weather, the banking system (no bank exists until someone founds one), a belief for every
  existing citizen (guilds and faith communities then form from them in the following seasons), named
  foreign citizens for each kingdom, territorial disputes and the military records. No coins are created: every new account starts empty. What was added is
  listed in `migrationNotes`. `tests/fixtures/m4-save.json` is a real save produced by the Milestone 4 code;
  tests migrate it, check every citizen and coin survives, and run it on.
- **A Milestone 3 village (version 4)** gains:
  - the road network, including roads for districts it has already opened;
  - the three kingdoms, funded from the outside account so no coins are created;
  - trade, diplomacy, the founding laws, culture and navigation.

  Every citizen, account and title is kept. `tests/fixtures/m3-save.json` is a real save produced by the
  Milestone 3 code, and a test migrates it and runs it for a year.
- **A Milestone 2 village** gains birth dates consistent with each person's age, sexes, family links,
  education, health, an archive, statistics and a government-ready council.
- **Milestone 1 saves** still migrate through the Milestone 2 path first.

Export and import use the same format. A reload continues identically even mid-journey, mid-unloading, with a
case pending, during road works, during a festival and across a season change. This is tested, and nothing is
duplicated.

### Deployment and the white-screen fix

The "white screen with words" on iPhone happened because GitHub Pages was publishing the raw repository
instead of the Vite build. **Settings → Pages → Source must be "GitHub Actions".**

- The deploy workflow runs the tests and checks that `dist/index.html` references
  `/kingdom-of-nobodies/assets/…` and never `/src/`. `tests/build.test.js` checks the same thing locally.
- `index.html` has an inline-styled startup screen that reloads once if assets are missing, then shows a clear
  message.
- If WebGL fails, a clear message appears while the simulation and panels keep working.
- The version and build ID are shown in the Council tab.
- There is no service worker, so there is no stale-cache trap.
- CI on pull requests runs the tests, the build, a Playwright mobile touch test on an emulated iPhone, and the
  200-year stress test.

### What the Milestone 5 stress test changed

- **Bank deposits were misreported.** The first runs showed deposits climbing to 340,000–735,000 coins,
  more money than the whole economy holds. The banks' books were sound: deposits matched the cash each bank
  held, and total money stayed fixed. The bug was in `financeSummary`: its running totals overwrote the
  current deposit figure, so it reported every coin ever deposited. This also made the Economy tab's
  "Saved in banks" figure wrong. Fixed, with a regression test.
- **Empty homes drew newcomers into joblessness.** The first runs had 5, 6, 28 and 19 hunger deaths (58 in
  total). Probes of the worst seeds found the deaths came during long stretches of 40–65% unemployment.
  Newcomers kept arriving because the planned streets always left beds to spare, and free beds counted in
  the town's appeal regardless of work. Free beds now attract only while there is work: their pull shrinks
  as unemployment rises and is gone at 50%. Hunger deaths fell to 0, 3, 33 and 3 (39 in total), and
  arrivals fell from about 1,000 to 750–840 per run. Seed 3 is worse than before (33 against 28). Changing
  migration sends each seed down a different history, so this is reported as is, not tuned away.

## Tuning decisions (Milestone 4)

The extended stress test surfaced real problems, and each was fixed at its cause rather than by switching a
system off:

- **Famine from unemployment.** In one seed, by year 50 only 25 of 90 adults had work. Businesses were
  closing and the pantry was empty. Children starved even though food was on sale, because their families
  had no coins. Two causes:
  - Owners of thriving businesses were emigrating "for a better life" and taking their households (and
    their businesses) with them. A household with an open business now never leaves for vague prospects,
    and savings over 90 coins weaken the pull abroad.
  - Newcomers kept arriving despite the joblessness. Unemployment now counts 1.6× against the village's
    appeal, and job openings attract outsiders only when locals aren't already out of work.
- **Districts opening too fast.** All ten districts were open by year 25 at 87 people. A new district now
  also needs population pressure: 10 + 7 × the districts already open, unless two or more villagers are
  homeless.
- **Diplomatic dead ends.** Some kingdoms stayed locked at −100 with each other, swapping embargoes forever.
  Now:
  - bitter relations fade toward indifference by 8% a season;
  - after an embargo is lifted, neither side may impose another for three years;
  - every active embargo costs the kingdom's leader approval;
  - a people tired of feuds elects a cooperative leader.
- **The distant world ran dry.** In the first full runs, the 50,000-coin "outside" purse (the wider world)
  was empty by year 60. Greenvale had hoarded 25,000 coins and the village 30,000, and the purse can only
  buy surpluses while it has coins. Once it was empty, Ironhold and Saltmere could no longer sell their
  surpluses, and they starved down to their population floor. Three changes:
  - prosperous kingdoms now spend 2% a day of the coins above their reserve on goods from distant lands;
  - villagers with more than 1,500 coins spend 1% a day of the excess with travelling pedlars;
  - the wider world starts with 250,000 coins.

  Milestone 3 saves receive a one-time top-up to that amount when they migrate, recorded in
  `migrationNotes`. Spending from more modest savings (above 200) was also tried. It sent the village's
  cash out of the valley and hunger deaths rose to 92–158 per 200 years, so it was rejected.
- **Trade economics.** Carriage costs 0.3 coins per hour of road. The distant world buys gluts cheaply and
  sells grain dearly (2.2×), and only when a kingdom has less than one day of food left. Regional trade is
  therefore the normal way food moves between settlements.
- **Diagnostics recorded in the save.** Every hunger death records its cause (no food to be had, a family too
  poor to buy food, out of work and penniless, stranded away from home). Every closed business records why
  it closed (bankrupt, unprofitable, owner died, owner emigrated, building destroyed).

## Known limitations

- Each kingdom has about 25 named people (rulers, nobles, merchants, families); the rest of its population
  is still simulated in aggregate. The 3D kingdom views are procedural towns built from the kingdom's state,
  not hand-made places, and you visit them as an observer.
- Warfare is resolved at the scale of armies and battles, with explained strength factors and a ±15% roll;
  there is no tactical control of units.
- Seasons and holidays (Milestone 5.1):
  - The visuals have been checked in Chromium with an emulated iPhone, using software WebGL. They have
    not been tried in Safari on a real iPhone.
  - Kingdom scenes are built when you arrive, so their crop fields show the season of your arrival until
    you visit again. Their trees take the season's colours but carry no blossom.
  - *Match my calendar* uses northern-hemisphere seasons.
  - The bells are synthesised, not recorded.
  - In the browser, an announced holiday is remembered for this device only, so another device announces
    it again.
- The cloud backup has been tested end-to-end only in-process (the Worker running against node:sqlite in
  the test suite). It is **not deployed**, so the shipped game shows cloud backup as "not set up".
- Unemployment can still run high in some seeds (around half of adults in one run). Emigration is the
  release valve, so migration churn is high: about 750 arrivals and 500 departures over 200 years.
- Hunger still kills: 2–10 people per 200 years per seed on the 5.1 code (25 over four seeds), mostly the
  frail and children of struggling families. Each death records its cause.
- The council's treasury is often low, so festivals are sometimes scaled down or cancelled.
- The simulation pauses while the page is closed. There is no offline catch-up.
- Planned streets are straight dirt streets branching at right angles from existing roads; there are no
  curved streets. The planner stops after 14 planned districts (about 103 plots), which every seed reached
  by year 50–75.
